geomagic AME state – deciphering Table I and Eq. (B1)

update: 2026-10-09 by W. Bruzda

The strings in the appendix are compact instructions for making the picture. Table I specifies which cells receive a fragment and which colors to use. Equation (B1) specifies where those fragments sit along the outer edge of a sector. To understand the recipe one can follow just one row of the table.

Throughout this page, a whole sector has area 1, so a whole hexagon has area 6. Row, column, sector and color labels all start at 0. A color is identified by its printed number as well as its appearance: 0 1 2 3 4 5.

Read each string digit by digit. For example, 430215 means the list 4, 3, 0, 2, 1, 5. Its first digit belongs to row $i=0$, its second to row $i=1$, and so on. A leading zero must be kept.

The words $\tau$ and $\sigma$ are permutations: each contains the labels $0$ – $5$ exactly once. The longer words in Eq. (B1) are orders of intervals, i.e., their matching labels deliberately repeat. A matching label $m$ is an entry in the table for the chosen sector $k$ and it is not a color label.

Thus the first instruction is: in cell (0, 4), sector $0$, add area $1/4$ in color $4$. The next instruction concerns cell $(1, 3)$, in the same sector, using color $2$. Continue down the six rows.

This set of six selected cells is called a matching (there is one cell in every row and every column). The color word assigns all six colors once to these cells. The table also chooses these matchings to meet each of the two displayed diagonals exactly once. Note that a table weight is an area contribution. Other records may add to the same coefficient. The weight $w$ is not a signed amplitude, and it is not yet a probability in the normalized quantum state.

Eq. (B1)

Now put the fragments in order. For sector 0, Eq. (B1) gives 23310102. Walk along the straight outer edge of that sector from its start to its end, as specified in Appendix B. This is left to right in the strip below. The eight consecutive intervals belong to matchings 2, 3, 3, 1, 0, 1, 0, 2. Matching $2$ appears first and last. In the canonical equal split, each interval is $1/8$ of the edge. For our selected cell $(0, 4)$, those two intervals are colored yellow, because its color label is $4$.

Join both endpoints of each interval to the hexagon's center. An interval occupying $1/8$ of the outer edge makes a triangle of area $1/8$ of the sector: all these triangles have the same altitude to that edge. The relevant width is the fraction of the straight edge, not the angle at the center.

For this particular coefficient, the two fragments are the complete contribution. Their areas add to

$A_{0404}=1/8+1/8=1/4$.

Both fragments are hatched in the paper's convention. Therefore

$U^{\rm g}_{4,4}=T^{\rm g}_{0404}=-\sqrt{1/4}=-1/2$.

The normalized quantum-state amplitude is this entry divided by six: $-1/12$. We sum the areas first and then take one signed square root. Taking a square root separately for each fragment would provide the wrong answer.

forming a hexagon

Fix just one of the small edge intervals. Its matching puts the corresponding triangle in six cells, i.e., one in each row, one in each column and one on each diagonal. Consequently, when the pieces of any one of those fourteen lines are assembled, that triangle appears exactly once.

Repeat this for every interval of all six sectors. The pieces cover the hexagon, with overlaps only along shared boundaries. The color permutations have a separate role. For every colored interval, its six copies use all six colors once. Hence, across the whole array, each color supplies one complete hexagon.

The particular orders in Eq. (B1) also help make the $36$ piece shapes different. A different ordering can preserve the areas and the tilings while changing the shapes. Pairwise non-congruence is the additional property checked in Appendix B and it does not follow just from adding the weights!

case of two occurrences with unequal widths

For one color word, the two positive widths need only add to $w$. In the main example, widths $3/16$ and $1/16$ would still give total area $1/4$ in each selected cell. These numbers are an illustration, not the actual widths chosen for Figure 1.

For two color words, the two widths must add to $2w$. Follow the matching's two occurrences in edge order, skipping the intervals belonging to other matchings. Allocate the first cumulative length $w$ to $\sigma_1$ and the remaining $w$ to $\sigma_2$. An occurrence may need an extra cut.

For example, with $w=1/16$, give the two occurrences widths $3/32$ and $1/32$. The first $2/32$ of the earlier occurrence uses $\sigma_1$. Its remaining $1/32$, together with all $1/32$ of the later occurrence, uses $\sigma_2$. Both color words still receive exactly $1/16$.

This changes the drawing while preserving every accumulated coefficient area. In the equal split the first occurrence uses $\sigma_1$ and the second uses $\sigma_2$. With unequal widths that shortcut need not hold.

table $\longrightarrow$ matrix

To recover the aggregate areas directly, start with every $A_{ijkl}$ equal to zero. For every record, and for each of its color words, run through rows $i=0,\ldots,5$ and add $w$ at the indicated address:

$A_{i,\tau(i),k,\sigma(i)}\leftarrow A_{i,\tau(i),k,\sigma(i)}+w$.

For a nonzero total, the matrix-entry magnitude is $\sqrt{A_{ijkl}}$. Table I and Eq. (B1) specify areas and placement. Signs come from Eq. (5), or from the hatching when reading the paper's picture. A zero total gives a zero entry. The matrix address is row $6i+j$, column $6k+l$.

The normalized state has amplitudes $T^{\rm g}_{ijkl}/6$, so its basis-state probabilities are $A_{ijkl}/36$. Equation (B1) affects how the same accumulated areas are drawn, not the resulting matrix entries.
